Kabale and Murchison Falls: A First-Hand Guide to Uganda’s Two Essential Destinations

Gateway to the gorillas in the southwest, Victoria Nile boat safaris in the northwest — and the roads, seasons, and realities that connect them.

On the paved highway between Butiru and Murchison Falls National Park, our vehicle slowed behind a white minibus whose cargo defied European physics. Stacked on the roof, reaching roughly twice the height of the vehicle itself, sat a mountain of mattresses, bundled bedding, and household goods, held in place by ropes and faith. Our Ugandan driver did not flinch. For him, this was Tuesday. For us — first-time visitors in October 2024 — it was the moment Uganda stopped being a destination on a map and became a real, operating country with its own logic of transport, commerce, and daily life.

I photographed that scene at GPS coordinates 1.9624°N, 31.7122°E, one of eight GPS-verified original images taken during a three-day visit in October 2024. Over five subsequent visits extending to June 2026, I covered the route from Entebbe through Kampala to both of Uganda’s anchor destinations: Kabale in the southwest, the staging town for gorilla trekking in Bwindi Impenetrable National Park, and Murchison Falls National Park in the northwest, home to the country’s most dramatic boat safari on the Victoria Nile. Every photograph, observation, and practical detail in this guide is drawn from those on-the-ground experiences.

This article covers both destinations in depth: what each offers, how to reach them, what to expect on the ground, and when to go. It also addresses two questions that rarely appear in standard travel guides — Uganda’s waste management licensing framework as it affects tourism facilities, and the specific seasonal considerations for travel in the southwest.

Murchison Falls: Boat Safaris, Game Drives, and the Power of the Nile

Murchison Falls National Park is Uganda’s largest protected area and the country’s most accessible destination for classic savannah wildlife viewing combined with a river experience found nowhere else in East Africa. The park straddles the Victoria Nile, which forces its entire volume through a gap barely seven metres wide before plunging 43 metres into the gorge below. That compression of water generates a roar and a mist plume visible from kilometres away.

During our October 2024 visit, we reached the falls by boat from the downstream launch site near Paraa. The journey upriver took approximately three hours, and the concentration of wildlife along the banks was immediate. Within the first twenty minutes, we spotted a massive Nile crocodile resting on the bank, partially submerged. Even from a safe distance aboard the boat, the animal’s size was striking — and our guide reminded us that crocodiles are deceptively fast, a detail that reframes every subsequent sighting.

Further upstream, an African elephant stood at the water’s edge, drinking or bathing while the surrounding forest provided a dense green backdrop. The encounter from the boat felt intimate in a way that a vehicle-based game drive does not: the water creates a natural buffer, the engine noise is low, and the animal’s behaviour appears largely undisturbed. On a separate game drive during the same visit, we encountered another elephant in the open savannah grassland — a large bull with prominent tusks, walking calmly through golden grass while his herd stood a short distance away. From the open-top safari vehicle, we were close enough to hear him breathing.

The Falls Themselves

The final stretch of the boat journey brings you to the base of Murchison Falls. The water becomes visibly rougher, the sound builds, and then the gorge opens to reveal the falls in full force. The sheer volume of water compressing through that narrow gap generates a power that is difficult to convey in photographs. The spray, the sound, the turbulence of the river below — it is genuinely one of the most impressive natural spectacles I have witnessed in East Africa. Our boat held position in the calmer water downstream while we watched and photographed. No one on the boat spoke for the first few minutes. The falls commanded silence.

Game Drives at Dawn

We departed our lodge before sunrise for a morning game drive, specifically timed to catch the dawn. The savannah at that hour is transformed: the sun breaks through acacia and palm silhouettes, painting the landscape in shades of orange and red that last only minutes. These sunrise moments — which I photographed at GPS coordinates 2.3703°N, 31.5493°E and 2.4331°N, 31.5109°E — are among the most visually striking experiences available in any Ugandan national park. The early hour also coincides with peak animal activity, particularly for herbivores and the predators that track them.

Kabale: Southwestern Uganda’s Gateway to the Gorillas

Kabale sits at approximately 1,870 metres elevation in Uganda’s southwestern highlands, surrounded by terraced hillsides and cool mountain air that feels nothing like the equatorial heat of Kampala. The town serves a dual function: it is the commercial and educational centre of the Kigezi region, and it is the last significant town before the gorilla trekking destinations of Bwindi Impenetrable National Park and Mgahinga Gorilla National Park.

For travellers, Kabale offers essential services that the remote lodges near Bwindi cannot always provide: banks with international ATMs, a covered market for provisions, a post office, mobile phone shops for SIM card purchases, and several fuel stations. The town also hosts multiple tourism training institutions, including the African College of Commerce and the Great Lakes Institute, reflecting Kabale’s growing role in building the skilled workforce that Uganda’s tourism industry requires. According to the KCCA Strategic Plan 2025, Uganda is investing in the training of tourism professionals and guides nationally — Kabale’s institutions are part of that pipeline.

Lake Bunyonyi and the Legacy of Leonard Sharp

Eight kilometres from Kabale lies Lake Bunyonyi, one of the most scenic bodies of water in East Africa. The lake, whose name means “place of many small birds” in the local Rukiga language, is surrounded by steeply terraced hillsides and dotted with 29 islands. It is deep — estimates range from 44 to over 900 metres, making it one of the deepest lakes in Africa — and it is one of very few lakes in the region free of bilharzia, which makes swimming safe.

The lake’s history includes a remarkable chapter of medical pioneering. In 1921, Leonard Sharp, a Scottish missionary and physician, established a hospital for leprosy patients on Bwama Island in Lake Bunyonyi. At a time when leprosy carried severe social stigma and patients were routinely isolated from their communities, Sharp created a treatment facility that offered both medical care and dignity. The hospital operated for decades and its legacy is still visible on the island today. Visiting Bwama Island by dugout canoe is one of the most meaningful side trips available from Kabale — it connects the traveller to a history that most gorilla trekking itineraries entirely overlook.

Mountain Gorillas: The Reason Kabale Exists on the Tourist Map

The mountain gorilla population in Uganda stood at approximately 459 individuals during the 2018–2020 census period. These animals, distributed across habituated and unhabituated groups in Bwindi Impenetrable National Park and Mgahinga Gorilla National Park, represent roughly half of the world’s remaining mountain gorilla population. The trekking permit system — USD 800 per person in peak season, with reduced rates from USD 450 during April, May, and November — funds the Uganda Wildlife Authority’s conservation operations, ranger salaries, and community revenue-sharing programmes.

During our January 2026 gorilla trekking experience, we were accompanied by a guide and two armed rangers. The rangers’ presence initially felt unusual — seeing firearms in a forest setting is not something European visitors expect — but it was entirely routine and reassuring. The rangers helped with the steep ascent through dense undergrowth, pointed out features of the forest, and maintained a professional calm that made the physically demanding trek feel safe throughout. Their expertise is the product of years of training and daily fieldwork in some of the most challenging terrain in East Africa.

When to Go: Seasons, Roads, and Practical Planning

Uganda’s southwest — the region around Kabale, Bwindi, and Lake Bunyonyi — is accessible year-round, but road conditions and trekking difficulty vary substantially by season. The two dry periods (June to September and December to February) offer the most comfortable travel: roads are firmer, trails inside the parks are less muddy, and the reduced humidity makes long treks more manageable. When I visited in October 2024, conditions were transitional — some rain, some dry stretches — and the roads were passable in a standard vehicle on the main routes, though a four-wheel drive was needed on the final approach to Murchison Falls.

The wet months of April, May, and November bring heavier rainfall that can turn unpaved roads into challenging terrain. A geländegängiges Fahrzeug — a proper four-wheel-drive vehicle with good ground clearance — becomes essential rather than optional. However, these low-season months carry a significant financial incentive: gorilla trekking permits may be available at reduced rates starting from USD 450, a substantial saving over the standard USD 800. Fewer tourists also mean quieter trails and a more intimate experience, though the physical demands are greater.

For Murchison Falls, the northern park is drier overall and the game drive tracks hold up better in rain. The boat safari operates year-round regardless of weather. Early morning game drives — the ones that deliver those extraordinary sunrise moments over the savannah — are possible in both dry and wet seasons, though cloud cover during the rains can obscure the sunrise effect.

Practical Logistics

The drive from Kampala to Kabale covers approximately 420 kilometres and takes seven to nine hours depending on traffic through Kampala and road conditions on the southwestern route. The Masaka Highway, a critical section of this journey, was under active reconstruction during our January 2026 visit — long stretches of sand and dust with heavy truck traffic. The drive from Kampala to Murchison Falls is shorter at roughly 305 kilometres and five to six hours.

Domestic flights connect Entebbe to airstrips near both destinations, cutting travel time dramatically but adding cost. For most travellers, the road journey is part of the experience — the overloaded minibuses, the roadside markets, the boda-boda riders carrying improbable cargo — and it provides context for the destinations themselves. Uganda is not a country you fly over; it is a country you drive through.

Environmental Regulation: What Operates Behind the Scenes

Tourism facilities in Uganda — lodges, camps, and hotels in protected areas — operate within a regulatory framework that most visitors never encounter but that shapes the sustainability of the industry. Under the National Environment (Waste Management) Regulations S.I. No. 49 of 2020, operators handling waste require a licence whose validity period is specified on the licence itself. Renewal applications must be submitted at least 60 days before expiry, and the licence is extended upon successful review.

This matters for travellers who care about the environmental footprint of their accommodation. Lodges operating inside or near national parks face specific waste management obligations because they generate waste in ecologically sensitive areas shared with wildlife. The regulatory framework, while imperfect in enforcement, provides a baseline standard that travellers can enquire about when choosing accommodation. Lodges that comply will know their licence status; those that do not may struggle to answer the question.

Uganda’s road infrastructure also falls under structured governance. A central City Road Safety Steering Committee in Kampala coordinates road safety management across agencies including the Kampala Capital City Authority, the Ministry of Works and Transport, the Uganda National Roads Authority, and the Uganda Police Force Traffic division (Kampala Road Safety Strategy 2021–2030). The condition of the roads that connect travellers to places like Kabale and Murchison Falls is not accidental — it reflects investment decisions, maintenance budgets, and the competing demands of a country that maintains approximately 500 kilometres of bitumen roads and 380 kilometres of gravel roads under the Uganda National Road Fund in Kampala alone (KCCA Ministerial Policy Statement 2017/18, figures require verification for current status).

Kampala itself generates an estimated 65 percent of Uganda’s national GDP, according to the Kampala Capital City Strategic Plan 2020/21–2024/25 (figure requires verification). The city’s strategic plan is anchored in Uganda Vision 2040, which targets the country’s transformation into a modern, prosperous nation. The Kampala City Roads and Bridges Upgrading Project, funded at EUR 250 million by UK Export Finance and the Ugandan government, represents the scale of infrastructure investment being directed at the road network that tourism depends on (KCCA Strategic Plan 2025).

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to visit southwestern Uganda for gorilla trekking?

The dry seasons (June–September and December–February) offer the most comfortable conditions. Wet months (April, May, November) require a four-wheel-drive vehicle but sometimes offer reduced permit rates from USD 450. The parks are open year-round.

How do I get from Kabale to Bwindi Impenetrable National Park?

Kabale is approximately 90 km from Bwindi’s Buhoma sector — about three hours by road on a mix of tarmac and unpaved surface. Most travellers arrange transport through their lodge or operator. A four-wheel drive is recommended in the rainy season.

What animals can you see on a Murchison Falls boat safari?

Nile crocodiles, hippos, African elephants, buffalo, waterbuck, and numerous waterbird species including African fish eagles and shoebill storks. Elephants frequently feed along the banks and crocodiles rest on sandbanks close to the boats.

How long is a waste management licence valid in Uganda?

Under S.I. No. 49 of 2020, the validity period is specified on each licence. Renewal must be applied for at least 60 days before expiry. This applies to tourism facilities operating in protected areas.

Is Kabale worth visiting beyond gorilla trekking?

Yes. Lake Bunyonyi (8 km away) offers 29 islands, safe swimming, and the historic Bwama Island where Leonard Sharp founded a leprosy hospital in 1921. Kabale also has markets, banks, and tourism training institutions serving Uganda’s growing hospitality sector.
